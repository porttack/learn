MATE ROV Competition: What It Builds

What this is. A locally built index of MATE ROV Competition: what the competition builds in students, inferred from the manuals (not tied to a year; read from the 2024 to 2026 manuals). Scope. This is a cross-reference catalog, not a standard. MATE publishes no standards of its own, so each entry is this project's inference of what the competition requires, scores and explains, written so a teacher can see what has to be taught and where. It was built from the 2024 Ranger, 2025 Scout, Navigator and Ranger and 2026 Scout manuals and the 2027 Floats description. Scope: Scout, Navigator and Ranger. Point totals, electrical limits, depths and timings change by year and class, so none is recorded as a competency; class differences are noted under each entry and compared in mate-classes.json. MATE's own list of 18 skills is mate-skills.json and each competency names the ones it develops. Codes. Codes are this project's: ENG (engineering practice), PRO (professional and team skills) and UND (ocean and environmental understanding), with a number. They are not MATE's. What this is not. The competition manuals are (c) MATE ROV Competition under a Creative Commons Attribution NonCommercial NoDerivatives license, so nothing here copies their text; every entry is a paraphrase written for this project, with manual section names given as references only.

This page is for a teacher who wants to know what the MATE ROV Competition is trying to build in students, which standards that work draws on, and where on this site it is taught. It covers the Scout, Navigator and Ranger classes. It is a cross-reference compiled here from MATE's manuals, not a standard and not tied to one year; MATE publishes no standards of its own.

1 of 26 competencies are linked to a lesson here so far, and 57 standards links are listed. A strength says how well the standard matches the competency, not how well any lesson teaches it.

What MATE says it builds

Skills for the workforce

The Overview opens by saying the competition develops technical talent and the skills employers want most: critical thinking, creativity, collaboration, time management and leadership, with data analytics, AI, machine learning and video marketing named alongside.

Overview, every manual from 2024 to 2026

Eighteen named skills

Icons throughout the manual mark the 18 Evaluate-Compete skills, nine employability and nine ROV-specific, that each requirement helps develop.

Overview icons, every manual

Think like an entrepreneur

Teams are asked to act as a start-up company with named roles (chief executive, finance, regulatory affairs, research and development, systems engineering, testing, operations, fundraising and marketing, and an ESG analyst), and to build business acumen, make financial decisions from data, critique design solutions, and reflect on their own strengths and weaknesses.

Overview, every manual

The ocean we want

Each year frames the work with the UN Ocean Decade, with phrases such as moving from the ocean we have to the ocean we want, the client being us, and doing good for good, and tags every mission task with Ocean Decade challenges.

Overview and Request for Proposals, every manual

Real science each year

Every Context & Need teaches real ocean science and why it matters: ocean observing and cables (2024), Great Lakes acidification, invasive species, offshore energy and shipwreck heritage (2025), the cryosphere, cold-water corals, eDNA, whale entanglement, iceberg risk, hybrid energy and floats that avoid ice (2026).

Context & Need, each year

Engineering reasoning

Judges want technical storytelling: evaluating alternatives, a work breakdown, constraints, a power budget, how sensors are calibrated, where code runs and how data flows, troubleshooting that is written down, and a project notebook.

Tips for effective written and oral communication, 2025 Ranger and 2026 Scout

Industry practice

Higher classes add practices from industry: a jobsite safety analysis with steps, hazards and safeguards in order of preference (Ranger), and scoring of teamwork, project management, problem solving and system design during the run (Navigator and Ranger).

Safety sections and scoring overview, 2025 Navigator and Ranger

Responsible use of AI

AI is welcome as a tool but not a crutch: describe how it was used, and do not use it to write the documentation or presentation.

Notes on the use of AI, 2026 Scout

The same kinds of task, every year

Tasks change yearly but come in families: recover objects, install or replace equipment, survey and measure, sample and analyze, and a separate device, the profiling float, which appears every year from 2024 to 2027 and gets harder.

Product demonstration, every manual

What judges reward

Scoring rubrics rate each item from missing to exceeds, award extra for novelty, depth of understanding and analysis, effectiveness and quality of implementation, and deduct for relying on outside help or purchased parts.

MATE scoring rubric for technical documentation and engineering presentation

ENG Engineering practice

#ENG.1 Turn a request for proposals into criteria and constraints

Read the mission request and the rules closely enough to say what the vehicle must do and what limits it, then decide what to build for the points available.

Where MATE shows it: Context & Need and Request for Proposals (every manual); Scoring overview (every manual)
MATE skills it develops: GEN.7 Critical Thinking and Problem SolvingGEN.3 Entrepreneurship
CA CTE (ICT)
  • C2.4 (partial) Understanding a customer's needs is a distinct analytical step in development.
    Why: Reading what the client actually asks for is the analysis step of design.
NGSS 2013
  • HS-ETS1-1 (strong) Take a large real-world challenge and turn it into specific requirements, both qualitative and quantitative, that take the needs of the people involved and the wider effects into account.
    Why: The request for proposals turns a large real-world need into specific requirements.
  • MS-ETS1-1 (strong) Spell out what a design problem has to achieve and what limits it (the criteria and constraints) in words precise enough that a solution can be tested against them, thinking about the people affected and the natural world.
    Why: Stating criteria and constraints precisely enough to test a design against them is the expectation itself.
Not yet linked to a lesson on this site.

#ENG.2 Compare design alternatives and justify the choice

Come up with more than one way to solve a problem, compare them against the criteria, and explain why one was chosen, including whether to build or buy.

MATE skills it develops: GEN.7 Critical Thinking and Problem SolvingGEN.2 CreativityROV.3 Structure
NGSS 2013
  • HS-ETS1-3 (strong) Judge a solution against prioritized criteria and the trade-offs among them (cost, safety, reliability, looks), including its social, cultural and environmental effects.
    Why: Judging a design on prioritized criteria and trade-offs such as cost, safety and reliability.
  • MS-ETS1-2 (strong) Compare several possible solutions to the same problem in a systematic way, judging how well each one meets the criteria and constraints.
    Why: Comparing design alternatives against criteria and constraints is the expectation itself.

The classes

Only the three classes the program teaches. Pioneer and Explorer, the college classes, are not covered. Class here is skill and experience level, not grade band: all three overlap in grades. The ladder compares what the manuals read (2025 Scout, Navigator and Ranger; 2026 Scout) require; limits and timings are from those years and change, so check the current manual.

SCOUT Scout

The starting class, for teams new to robotics, recommended for elementary, middle and beginning high schools and open to grades K to 12, with elementary teams needing their regional coordinator's approval.

NAVIGATOR Navigator

The next step, recommended for middle schools with robotics experience, high schools new to robotics and advanced elementary schools, open to grades 5 to 12.

RANGER Ranger

For middle and high school teams with robotics experience and a good grasp of robotic systems and electricity, open to grades 5 to 12, with teams taking part in a regional event.
