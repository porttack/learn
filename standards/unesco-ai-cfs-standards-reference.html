UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students Reference

What this is. A locally built index of UNESCO AI Competency Framework for Students (2024) (2024). Codes. Codes are UNESCO's own: 4.<level>.<dimension>, where level 1=Understand, 2=Apply, 3=Create. Each competency also has 2-3 curricular goals coded CG4.<level>.<dimension>.<n> in the source document; those aren't indexed here, only the 12 top-level competencies. What this is not. #Human-centred mindset

  • #4.1.1 Human agency (Understand) AI is built and controlled by humans, and understanding what it means for AI to stay human-controlled, and what happens when it isn't, is the starting point for understanding AI's impact on rights and daily life.

    Unassigned

  • #4.2.1 Human accountability (Apply) AI creators and service providers carry real legal and social obligations, and human choice should never be fully handed over to AI for high-stakes decisions.

    Unassigned

  • #4.3.1 AI society citizenship (Create) Being a responsible citizen in an AI-driven society means critically examining how AI affects society, including bias and inequality, and taking an active role in pushing AI development toward inclusive and just outcomes.

    Unassigned

#Ethics of AI

  • #4.1.2 Embodied ethics (Understand) Before using AI tools, students should understand core ethical principles like not causing harm, weighing whether a use is actually justified, watching for bias, protecting privacy, and knowing humans stay responsible rather than machines.

    Unassigned

  • #4.2.2 Safe and responsible use (Apply) Using AI responsibly means following ethical principles and local regulations, understanding the real risk of exposing your data, and knowing how to protect your own and others' safety.

    Unassigned

  • #4.3.2 Ethics by design (Create) Building ethics into an AI tool from the start, rather than adding it afterward, means examining every stage of the tool's life cycle and judging whether it actually complies with ethical and legal rules.

    Unassigned

#AI techniques and applications

  • #4.1.3 AI foundations (Understand) Building real AI literacy starts with basic knowledge of data and algorithms, and connecting that knowledge to how AI shows up in everyday and social life.

    Unassigned

  • #4.2.3 Application skills (Apply) Using AI tools well takes real technical skill, including being able to evaluate and use free or open-source AI tools, libraries, and datasets critically rather than just accepting them.

    Unassigned

  • #4.3.3 Creating AI tools (Create) At the most advanced level, students apply their knowledge of data and algorithms to customize existing AI tools and build new, task-specific ones, while still applying ethical judgment to that work.

    Unassigned

#AI system design

  • #4.1.4 Problem scoping (Understand) Before trying to solve a problem with AI, students should be able to judge whether AI is even the right tool, from a legal, ethical, and logical standpoint, and define the problem's real boundaries and goals.

    Unassigned

  • #4.2.4 Architecture design (Apply) Building a working AI system means understanding how data, algorithms, models, and interfaces fit together into something scalable and maintainable, not just functional once.

    Unassigned

  • #4.3.4 Iteration and feedback loops (Create) Evaluating and improving an AI system over time, including knowing when it should be shut down, combines technical ability with ethical judgment.

    Unassigned
