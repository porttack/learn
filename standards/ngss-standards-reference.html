Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS 2013) Reference

What this is. A locally built index of Next Generation Science Standards (NGSS Lead States, 2013), performance expectations (2013). Scope. Only the performance expectations this program's ocean-robotics material can honestly touch, not all of NGSS: the eight Engineering Design expectations (MS-ETS1-1 to 4 and HS-ETS1-1 to 4) that MATE ROV work and the CS50 float problems practice, plus two Earth and space science expectations (MS-ESS2-6, HS-ESS2-4) about ocean heat and climate that the Argo float background in Profiling Float touches. Not indexed: the Physical Science expectations on forces, energy, waves and circuits, and the Life Science and Earth science expectations about ocean ecosystems, which the ROV lesson cards would need if they are ever aligned; add them then. The three NGSS dimensions behind each expectation (science and engineering practices, disciplinary core ideas, crosscutting concepts) are not indexed separately. Codes. Codes are NGSS's own performance expectation codes (grade band, topic, number), used as published. NGSS does not name its expectations; the items here have no `name`, only a paraphrase. Groups are this project's own, by grade band and topic. What this is not. #Middle School: Engineering Design (MS-ETS1)

  • #MS-ETS1-1 Spell out what a design problem has to achieve and what limits it (the criteria and constraints) in words precise enough that a solution can be tested against them, thinking about the people affected and the natural world.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float) (partial)
      Profiling Float: the criteria come from the competition task (stay within 0.33 m of the hold depth for 30 seconds in a row, do not touch the bottom), so the student does not define them. The student does turn them into precise, testable rules, in_band and held, which is the testable-criteria half of this expectation.
  • #MS-ETS1-2 Compare several possible solutions to the same problem in a systematic way, judging how well each one meets the criteria and constraints.

    Unassigned

  • #MS-ETS1-3 Read the data from tests of several solutions to see what each one does well or badly, and use that to pull the best features of each into a better design.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float)
      Profiling Float: the student reads the data packets of three simulated floats, one fine and two flawed, and decides which meets the criteria and why the others fail. That is the analyze-test-data half of this expectation. The combine-the-best-features half is only touched by the Going Further 'Fix the floats' item.
  • #MS-ETS1-4 Build and use a model of a proposed object, tool or process, and use the data from testing the model to improve the design again and again.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float), Unit 14 (Profiling Float: PID) (partial)
      Both problems run a student's program against a simulated pool and use the results to improve it. Profiling Float: the simulator is given, and the iteration is light (steer, check the three floats). Profiling Float: PID: the iteration is the point (tune kp, ki, kd and write down what each change did; fix the windup bug found by testing), which would be strong on its own. The student uses a model here but does not build one.

#High School: Engineering Design (HS-ETS1)

  • #HS-ETS1-1 Take a large real-world challenge and turn it into specific requirements, both qualitative and quantitative, that take the needs of the people involved and the wider effects into account.

    Unassigned

  • #HS-ETS1-2 Break a complex problem into smaller, more manageable problems that each can be solved with engineering.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float) (related)
      Profiling Float is split for the student into four small functions (steer, touched the bottom, in the band, held) that together judge a float. The splitting is given, not something the student works out.
  • #HS-ETS1-3 Judge a solution against prioritized criteria and the trade-offs among them (cost, safety, reliability, looks), including its social, cultural and environmental effects.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float), Unit 14 (Profiling Float: PID) (related)
      Both judge a design against stated pass criteria (hold 30 seconds, stay off the bottom, score +5 and -5), and Going Further asks whether the best tuning is one to trust in a real pool. The criteria are not prioritized against each other and no cost, safety or environmental trade-off is asked for, so this is adjacent only.
  • #HS-ETS1-4 Use a computer simulation to see how a proposed solution behaves across the range of conditions it could face, as a way to test and refine it.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float), Unit 14 (Profiling Float: PID) (partial)
      A computer simulation of a float in a pool is the test bench for both problems. Profiling Float: PID is closest: its simulator adds a constant bias, sensor noise and a slow motor, and a controller passes only if it holds in all nine pools, so it is tested across a range of conditions. The simulator is given, not built, and it models one float, not a system with many interacting criteria.

#Earth and Space Science: Ocean, Heat and Climate

  • #MS-ESS2-6 Use a model of how the Sun heats the Earth unevenly, and how the Earth spins, to explain the patterns of air and ocean circulation that give different regions their climates.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float) (related)
      Profiling Float's Argo background and explorer let students compare temperature against depth for floats in different oceans. No model of circulation or regional climate is asked for; this is context only.
  • #HS-ESS2-4 Use a model to describe how changes in the flow of energy into and out of Earth's systems (the ocean among them) can change the climate.
    • CS50 Problems: Unit 13 (Profiling Float) (related)
      The background says the ocean takes up most of the extra heat from global warming and that floats are how it is watched. Context only: nothing asks the student to model energy flow or climate.
