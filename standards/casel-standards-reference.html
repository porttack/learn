CASEL Social-Emotional Learning Standards Reference

What this is. A locally built index of CASEL's SEL Framework (Collaborative for Academic, Social, and Emotional Learning), the "CASEL wheel," 2020 revision (2020). Scope. CASEL is a general social-and-emotional-learning framework, not a CS-specific one -- it's cataloged here as an orthogonal standard a lesson can also be checked against, alongside the CS/digital-literacy frameworks above. The sub-competency list below reflects CASEL's current (2020-revision) wheel, confirmed directly against casel.org on the extraction date; an earlier (January 2017) version of the wheel used a shorter, less equity-explicit list under each competency, so a secondary source describing CASEL's sub-competencies may not match this file if it's citing that older wheel. Codes. Codes are this project's own sequential numbering, not CASEL's: <prefix>.<n> per competency, in the order CASEL's own wheel lists them within that competency (SA=Self-Awareness, SM=Self-Management, SOC=Social Awareness, REL=Relationship Skills, RDM=Responsible Decision-Making). CASEL does not number or code its sub-competencies itself, only names them, the same situation unesco-mil was in. What this is not. CASEL's SEL Framework (c) CASEL, all rights reserved; CASEL's own terms permit noncommercial use in preschool-through-high-school education, which is the only use made of it here. The five competency names and the official sub-competency names are CASEL's; every paraphrase here is written for this project, never CASEL's own text. See also. #Self-Awareness

Understanding your own emotions, values, and identity well enough to see how they shape your behavior, and seeing yourself accurately with a confident, growth-oriented outlook.

  • #SA.1 Integrating personal and social identities A student's sense of self blends their personal identity with the social identities, family, culture, community, they're part of, not one or the other alone.
    • porttack.com: SEL: Words That Built Our Classroom Community
      The word list itself blends personal traits (funny, determined, artistic) with social/cultural identities (italian, mexican, polish, american, christian, sister, brother) in the same brainstorm -- this competency's own definition, not an inference.
  • #SA.2 Identifying personal, cultural, and linguistic assets Recognizing the strengths that come from a student's own background, culture, and language is part of self-awareness, not something separate from it.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.3 Identifying one's emotions Naming what you're actually feeling, accurately and specifically, is the starting point for managing it.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.4 Demonstrating honesty and integrity Being self-aware includes being honest with yourself and others about who you are and what you value.
    • Little Brother (a book by Cory Doctorow): Chapter 16 (A Confession to His Parents)
      Confessing everything to his parents -- 'I came clean... holding in the secret had dirtied me, soiled my spirit. It had made me afraid and ashamed' -- is this standard's own claim that self-awareness includes being honest with the people closest to you, not just with yourself.
    • porttack.com: Society (Impact of Computing): Can a Company Have a Conscience? (partial)
      The post's closing line, "this is how we show students that character counts," is a direct appeal to honesty and integrity as self-aware values, even though the lesson's content is a corporate ethics case study, not a personal-identity exercise.
  • #SA.5 Linking feelings, values, and thoughts Noticing how an emotion, a belief, and a thought connect to each other is what makes self-awareness more than just naming a feeling in isolation.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.6 Examining prejudices and biases Looking honestly at your own prejudices and biases, rather than assuming you don't have any, is part of understanding how your thinking shapes your behavior.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.7 Experiencing self-efficacy Believing you're capable of doing something well is what lets a student actually attempt it.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.8 Having a growth mindset Treating ability as something that develops with effort, not a fixed trait, changes how a student responds to a setback.

    Unassigned

  • #SA.9 Developing interests and a sense of purpose Knowing what you care about and why gives direction to the choices you make.

    Unassigned

#Self-Management

Regulating your own emotions, thoughts, and behavior well enough to work toward a goal, even under stress.

  • #SM.1 Managing one's emotions Regulating a strong emotion in the moment, rather than being controlled by it, is a learnable skill.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.2 Identifying and using stress management strategies Recognizing stress and having a real strategy for it, not just getting through it, is part of self-management.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.3 Exhibiting self-discipline and self-motivation Following through on something without someone else pushing you to is what separates self-management from just complying.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.4 Setting personal and collective goals Setting a goal for yourself and setting one for a group you're part of are related but separate skills, both part of self-management.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.5 Using planning and organizational skills Breaking a goal into steps and keeping track of them is what turns an intention into a finished result.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.6 Showing the courage to take initiative Starting something before being told to, even when the outcome is uncertain, takes a kind of courage that's part of self-management.

    Unassigned

  • #SM.7 Demonstrating personal and collective agency Believing your own actions, and your group's actions together, can actually change an outcome is what makes effort feel worthwhile.

    Unassigned

#Social Awareness

Understanding and empathizing with people whose backgrounds, cultures, and circumstances differ from your own, including the norms and systems that shape their situation.

  • #SOC.1 Taking others' perspectives Genuinely considering how a situation looks from someone else's point of view, not just assuming your own view is universal.
    • porttack.com: Society (Impact of Computing): Prove Me Wrong: A Socratic Seminar on AI and Cheating
      The whole design -- inquiry first, teacher opinion held back until the reveal, a before/after temperature check scored by who changed the most minds -- exists specifically to get students taking each other's perspectives seriously, not performing a position.
    • porttack.com: Socratic Seminar Kit: Socratic Seminar Kit, The Rules Students Wrote: A Socratic Seminar on the STUDENTS FIRST Act, Can Robotic AI Teachers Replace Human Teachers? A Socratic Seminar, You Deleted It. Is It Gone? A Socratic Seminar on Doorbell Cameras and AI Chats, Who Sets the Rules for AI? A Socratic Seminar on a Student Survey
      The opening and closing temperature checks and the fishbowl's hot seat are built specifically to get students taking each other's perspectives seriously -- the run of show's whole shape, not any one seminar's content.
  • #SOC.2 Recognizing strengths in others Noticing what someone else is good at is part of social awareness, not just noticing their emotions.

    Unassigned

  • #SOC.3 Demonstrating empathy and compassion Empathy is understanding what someone else feels; compassion is caring enough to want to help.
    • porttack.com: SEL: Words That Built Our Classroom Community (partial)
      Reading and reacting to classmates' words during the art-making phase is a light touch of empathy/compassion, secondary to the identity work the lesson is actually built around.
  • #SOC.4 Showing concern for the feelings of others Paying attention to how your words or actions land on someone else is a basic form of social awareness.

    Unassigned

  • #SOC.5 Understanding and expressing gratitude Recognizing when someone has helped you, and saying so, is part of being socially aware, not just being polite.

    Unassigned

  • #SOC.6 Identifying diverse social norms, including unjust ones Different settings have different unwritten rules for behavior, and some of those rules are themselves unfair, worth noticing rather than just following.

    Unassigned

  • #SOC.7 Recognizing situational demands and opportunities Reading what a specific situation actually calls for, rather than applying one fixed script everywhere, is part of social awareness.

    Unassigned

  • #SOC.8 Understanding the influences of organizations and systems on behavior People's behavior is shaped by the institutions and systems around them, not just by personal choice alone.

    Unassigned

#Relationship Skills

Building and keeping healthy relationships across a range of people and groups, through clear communication, cooperation, and constructive conflict.

  • #REL.1 Communicating effectively Getting your actual meaning across, and listening well enough to receive someone else's, is the foundation every other relationship skill builds on.
    • porttack.com: Society (Impact of Computing): Prove Me Wrong: A Socratic Seminar on AI and Cheating
      The fishbowl format -- one voice at a time, building on what came before, citing evidence -- is a structured implementation of communicating effectively, not just a discussion format left to chance.
    • porttack.com: Socratic Seminar Kit: Socratic Seminar Kit, The Rules Students Wrote: A Socratic Seminar on the STUDENTS FIRST Act, Can Robotic AI Teachers Replace Human Teachers? A Socratic Seminar, You Deleted It. Is It Gone? A Socratic Seminar on Doorbell Cameras and AI Chats, Who Sets the Rules for AI? A Socratic Seminar on a Student Survey
      The fishbowl -- one voice at a time, build on what came before, cite the text -- is a structured implementation of communicating effectively built into the format itself, not left to chance by whatever topic fills it.
  • #REL.2 Developing positive relationships Building a relationship that's actually healthy takes deliberate effort, not just spending time near someone.
    • Little Brother (a book by Cory Doctorow): Chapter 13 (A Secret Comes Out), Chapter 17 (Barbara Stratford and the Crypto Wars)
      Van's deliberate trade of secrets with Marcus -- 'I won't swear you to secrecy because what's the point? Either I can trust you or I can't,' followed by her own confession about the stolen standardized tests (13) -- and Ange's 'you and I, we're together now' commitment once she realizes she shares Marcus's risk (17) are both this standard's claim that a real relationship takes deliberate effort, not just proximity.
  • #REL.3 Demonstrating cultural competency Working well across cultural differences means knowing enough to engage respectfully, not just being polite in the abstract.

    Unassigned

  • #REL.4 Practicing teamwork and collaborative problem-solving Solving a problem as a team is a different skill from solving the same problem alone, and it has to be practiced on its own.

    Unassigned

  • #REL.5 Resolving conflicts constructively Working through a disagreement so both sides actually come out better, instead of just ending the argument, is a specific skill.

    Unassigned

  • #REL.6 Resisting negative social pressure Saying no to pressure from a peer group takes a skill of its own, separate from knowing the right answer.

    Unassigned

  • #REL.7 Showing leadership in groups Leading a group well means helping it function better, not just being the one in charge.

    Unassigned

  • #REL.8 Seeking or offering support and help when needed Knowing when to ask for help, and when to offer it, is a relationship skill most people have to learn deliberately.
    • Little Brother (a book by Cory Doctorow): Chapter 17 (Barbara Stratford and the Crypto Wars)
      Darryl's father telling his own story and crying 'unashamed' in front of the other families gathered at Barbara Stratford's apartment is grief processed with support actively sought and offered, not carried alone.
  • #REL.9 Standing up for the rights of others Speaking up when someone else is being treated unfairly, even when it doesn't affect you directly, is part of maintaining a healthy relationship with a community.

    Unassigned

#Responsible Decision-Making

Making a thoughtful choice about your own behavior and social interactions by weighing evidence, ethics, safety, and the well-being of everyone affected.

  • #RDM.1 Demonstrating curiosity and open-mindedness Being willing to ask a real question and consider an answer you didn't expect is what makes a decision actually informed.

    Unassigned

  • #RDM.2 Learning how to make a reasoned judgment after analyzing information, data, and facts Working from evidence to a conclusion, rather than from a gut reaction, is the core move of responsible decision-making.
    • porttack.com: Society (Impact of Computing): Prove Me Wrong: A Socratic Seminar on AI and Cheating
      "Cite the text or a real example when you make a claim" (one of the five posted norms) and the observer organizer's evidence columns are this standard's reasoned-judgment-from-evidence made into a gradable classroom routine.
    • porttack.com: Socratic Seminar Kit: Socratic Seminar Kit, The Rules Students Wrote: A Socratic Seminar on the STUDENTS FIRST Act, Can Robotic AI Teachers Replace Human Teachers? A Socratic Seminar, You Deleted It. Is It Gone? A Socratic Seminar on Doorbell Cameras and AI Chats, Who Sets the Rules for AI? A Socratic Seminar on a Student Survey
      "Cite the text or a real example when you make a claim" (one of the five posted norms) and the observer organizer's evidence columns make reasoned, evidence-based judgment a gradable routine of the format, regardless of which seminar is running.
  • #RDM.3 Identifying solutions for personal and social problems Generating real options for a problem, not just naming that a problem exists, is where responsible decision-making actually does its work.

    Unassigned

  • #RDM.4 Anticipating and evaluating the consequences of one's actions Thinking ahead to what a choice will actually lead to, for yourself and for other people, before making it.

    Unassigned

  • #RDM.5 Recognizing how critical thinking skills are useful both inside and outside of school Critical thinking taught in a classroom is meant to transfer to decisions made outside it, not stay confined to schoolwork.

    Unassigned

  • #RDM.6 Reflecting on one's role to promote personal, family, and community well-being Looking back at how your own choices affected the people and communities around you, not just yourself, is part of making better ones next time.

    Unassigned

  • #RDM.7 Evaluating personal, interpersonal, community, and institutional impacts A single decision can ripple out from an individual to a relationship to a whole community or institution, and responsible decision-making means weighing all of those levels, not just the nearest one.

    Unassigned
